Touch / Music / Light / 2021

MoodBall

Squeeze it, move it, or hold it. MoodBall responds with music, light, and moving projections. Our team explored how a pair of these balls could help people share a feeling, even when they were apart.

01 / Brief

Sharing a feeling without finding the words.

We began with Connection, a music installation where each person could add an instrument or melody. As more people joined in, the sound and coloured light grew together.

Feedback on the proposal led us to something smaller for homes and classrooms. We kept the idea of sharing music, but put it inside a ball. A second ball could echo its response for a friend or family member elsewhere.

04 / Evaluation

Does a squeeze mean the same thing to everyone?

In our TEST001 study, five people listened to music, matched it to moods, and tried gestures with the ball. We wanted to find out what felt natural and what was confusing before continuing the build.

Before the session, we explained how we would use the information and that personal details would stay private. Everyone could stop at any time.

01 / MAPPING

The same movement meant different things.

People did not always agree on which mood a gesture expressed. The ball could suggest a feeling, but it could not know for sure.

02 / CALIBRATION

Some people needed a lighter touch.

At the exhibit, some children and teenagers could not press hard enough to trigger the response they wanted.

03 / DESIGN RESPONSE

Let people choose what they share.

A next version should adjust to each person’s touch and let them change the suggested mood before sharing it.

MoodBall is an experiment in expression and play. It does not measure or diagnose someone’s emotional state.

What we used

  • Pressure sensors for force and gesture intensity
  • GSR sensor for skin conductance input
  • Accelerometer and gyroscope for movement
  • Arduino for signal collection and formatting
  • Unity for projected visual response
  • LED and speaker feedback inside the object

06 / Outcome

What happened when people tried it.

The finished prototype brought pressure, motion, and skin conductance sensors together with music, LEDs, and Unity projections. Visitors quickly began trying different movements to see what sounds and colours they could create.

We also found plenty to improve. Daylight made the lights and projections harder to see. The pressure settings did not suit every age, and people were more interested in the ball in front of them than its remote connection. I would next focus on adjusting the response to each person and making the sharing controls clearer.

WORKED

People could see and hear their actions.

Moving or squeezing the ball could change the music, colour, or projection around it.

FRICTION

Younger visitors needed lighter pressure.

Lighter pressure made some modes difficult to trigger for younger visitors.

NEXT

Make it personal before connecting two balls.

Let each person adjust the sensitivity, change a suggested mood, and choose what to send.
